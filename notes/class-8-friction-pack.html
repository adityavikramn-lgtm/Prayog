1. What is friction?

Friction is a force that opposes motion (or an attempt to move) between two surfaces that touch each other. It acts along the surfaces, opposite to the direction of motion.

Why does it happen? Even smooth-looking surfaces have tiny bumps. When two surfaces press together, the bumps lock into each other.

2. Why is the first push the hardest?

The friction that stops a still object from moving (static friction) is more than the friction while it slides (sliding friction). When still, the bumps have time to lock firmly; when sliding, they don't.

Example A 20 kg box needs 100 N to start moving but only 60 N to keep it moving at a steady speed.
Remember static friction > sliding friction > rolling friction. That's why wheels make moving things easy.

3. What decides how much friction there is?

  • The type of surfaces: rough surfaces (sandpaper, gravel) have more friction than smooth ones (glass, ice).
  • How hard they are pressed together: a heavier box has more friction.

4. Friction: a necessary evil

Useful: we need it to

  • walk without slipping
  • hold a pen and write
  • stop a bicycle with brakes
  • light a matchstick

Harmful: it also

  • wears out shoe soles and tyres
  • heats up machine parts
  • wastes energy in engines

5. Increasing and reducing friction

To increase frictionTo reduce friction
treads on tyres and shoe solesoil or grease (lubricants)
gymnasts' chalk powder on handsball bearings in fans and cycles
sand on a slippery roadwheels on suitcases

Fluid friction (drag): air and water also oppose motion. Fish, birds and aeroplanes have streamlined shapes to reduce it.

Common mistake Saying friction always "stops" things. It only opposes relative motion. Without friction, your feet couldn't push the ground to walk!
