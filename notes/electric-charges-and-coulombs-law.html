1What is electric charge?

Rub a plastic comb on dry hair and it starts picking up bits of paper. Something has changed on the comb. We say it has become charged. Electric charge is a basic property of matter, just like mass. Some particles have it, some don't, and it is what makes electric forces happen.

There are exactly two kinds of charge. Benjamin Franklin named them positive (+) and negative (−). The names are just labels. What matters is how they behave:

Remember
  • Like charges repel. Unlike charges attract.
  • The SI unit of charge is the coulomb (C).
  • In everyday problems, charges are usually tiny: 1 μC=10−61\ \mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}1 μC=10−6 C and 1 nC=10−91\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}1 nC=10−9 C.

Where does charge come from?

Every atom has a small heavy nucleus (protons and neutrons) with electrons around it. Protons carry positive charge, electrons carry an equal amount of negative charge, and neutrons carry none.

ParticleChargeMass
Electron−e=−1.6×10−19-e = -1.6 \times 10^{-19}−e=−1.6×10−19 C9.1×10−319.1 \times 10^{-31}9.1×10−31 kg

Neutron0001.67×10−271.67 \times 10^{-27}1.67×10−27 kg

A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons, so the charges cancel. A body becomes negatively charged when it gains extra electrons, and positively charged when it loses some. Only electrons move around in everyday charging. The protons stay locked in the nucleus.

Common slip

A positively charged body has lost electrons. It has not gained protons.

2Properties of electric charge

1. Charge is additive (it's a scalar)

The total charge of a system is just the algebraic sum of all the charges in it, with their signs. For example, charges +3+3+3 C, −5-5−5 C and +1+1+1 C add up to −1-1−1 C. Charge has no direction, so it is a scalar.

2. Charge is quantised

Charge always comes in whole-number multiples of the smallest charge, eee. You can't have half an electron's charge on a body.

Quantisation of charge
q=± ne,n=1,2,3,…,e=1.6×10−19 Cq = \pm\, n e, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots, \qquad e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}q=±ne,n=1,2,3,…,e=1.6×10−19 C

Because eee is so small, quantisation is invisible in everyday life. A charge of just 1 μC1\ \mu\text{C}1 μC is already about 6×10126 \times 10^{12}6×1012 electrons, so charge looks continuous.

3. Charge is conserved

Charge can't be created or destroyed. It can only move from one body to another. When you rub glass with silk, the glass loses exactly as many electrons as the silk gains. The total charge of an isolated system never changes.

4. Charge is invariant

The charge on a particle is the same no matter how fast it moves. (Mass, by contrast, changes at speeds close to light.)

3Conductors, insulators and earthing

  • Conductors let charge move through them easily, because some electrons are free to wander. Metals, the human body and the Earth are conductors.
  • Insulators hold charge where it is put. Their electrons are tightly bound. Glass, plastic, rubber, dry wood and silk are insulators.
  • Earthing (grounding) means connecting a conductor to the Earth. The Earth is so big that it can give or take any amount of charge, so an earthed conductor shares away its extra charge.
Why it matters

Charge given to a conductor spreads out over its outer surface. Charge given to an insulator stays at the spot where it was placed.

4Three ways to charge a body

1. By friction (rubbing)

Rubbing two different materials moves electrons from one to the other. Both end up with equal and opposite charges.

Rub this……with thisResult
Glass rodSilkGlass +++, silk −-−
Ebonite (or plastic) rodFur or woolEbonite −-−, fur +++

2. By conduction (touching)

Touch a charged body to a neutral conductor and some charge flows across. The neutral body ends up with the same sign of charge, and the first body loses some of its charge.

3. By induction (without touching)

This is the clever one. You charge a conductor without ever touching it with the charged object:

  1. Bring a positive rod near a neutral metal sphere. The sphere's free electrons are pulled towards the rod, so the near side becomes negative and the far side positive.
  2. Earth the sphere. Electrons flow up from the Earth and cancel the positive charge on the far side.
  3. Remove the earth connection first, then take the rod away.
  4. The extra electrons spread over the whole sphere. It is now negatively charged.
Induction in one line

Induction always gives the body the opposite sign to the charge that induced it, and the inducing body loses nothing.

Order matters

If you take the rod away before removing the earth, the electrons simply flow back to the Earth and the sphere ends up neutral.

5Coulomb's law

In 1785 Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured the force between charged bodies with a torsion balance. He found a simple rule:

Coulomb's law in words

The force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. It acts along the line joining them.

Coulomb's law
F=k q1q2r2,k=14πε0=9×109 N m2/C2F = k\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 9 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2\text{/C}^2F=kr2q1​q2​​,k=4πε0​1​=9×109 N m2/C2

ε0=8.85×10−12 C2/(N m2)\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2\text{/(N m}^2)ε0​=8.85×10−12 C2/(N m2) is the permittivity of free space. (More precisely k=8.99×109k = 8.99 \times 10^9k=8.99×109; JEE uses 9×1099 \times 10^99×109.)

Reading the formula

  • Product of charges. Double one charge and the force doubles. Double both and it becomes four times as big.
  • Inverse square. Double the distance and the force drops to one quarter, not one half. Halve the distance and it becomes four times as big.
  • Sign tells the type. Put the charges in with their signs: a positive answer means repulsion, a negative answer means attraction. (For the size of the force, use the magnitudes.)

When does it apply?

  • To point charges, meaning bodies much smaller than the distance between them.
  • To charges at rest (that's why this topic is called electrostatics).

8Newton's third law and the superposition principle

The force q1q_1q1​ puts on q2q_2q2​ is always equal and opposite to the force q2q_2q2​ puts on q1q_1q1​, even if the charges are different sizes. Both use the same product q1q2q_1q_2q1​q2​ in Coulomb's law.

Superposition principle

When many charges act on one charge, each pair interacts as if the others were not there. The net force is the vector sum of the separate forces:

F⃗net=F⃗1+F⃗2+F⃗3+…\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \vec F_3 + \dotsFnet​=F1​+F2​+F3​+…

How to use it, every time: (1) find the size of each force with Coulomb's law; (2) draw its direction (away for like charges, towards for unlike); (3) add the arrows as vectors.

9Where is the net force zero? (Equilibrium)

A very common JEE question: two fixed charges, and you must find where a third charge would feel no net force. This point is called the null point.

Where to look
  • Like charges (both + or both −): the null point is between them, closer to the smaller charge.
  • Unlike charges: the null point is outside, on the side of the smaller charge (beyond it).
  • The size and sign of the test charge don't matter. It cancels out of the equation.

13Common mistakes to avoid

  • Forgetting to convert units. μC\mu\text{C}μC to C (×10−6\times 10^{-6}×10−6) and cm to m (×10−2\times 10^{-2}×10−2) before using k=9×109k = 9 \times 10^9k=9×109.
  • Forgetting to square rrr. The force goes as 1/r21/r^21/r2, not 1/r1/r1/r.
  • Adding forces as plain numbers. Forces are vectors. Add them as arrows, unless they lie on one line.
  • Thinking a bigger charge feels a bigger force. In a pair, both charges feel the same size of force.
  • Searching for the null point on the wrong side. Like charges: between them. Unlike charges: outside, beyond the smaller one.
  • Sharing charge without signs. +8+8+8 and −2-2−2 share to +3+3+3 each, not +5+5+5.
  • Using the medium wrongly. In a medium the force is F/KF/KF/K, smaller than in vacuum.
